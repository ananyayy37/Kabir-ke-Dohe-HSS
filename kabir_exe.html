01 — WHY KABIR.EXE

The other kind of debugging

We spend a surprising amount of time debugging code — tracing a fault back through a stack of function calls until we find the one broken line. We rarely apply the same patience to ourselves.

This project treats two dohas (rhymed couplets) of the fifteenth-century poet-saint Kabir Das as two small diagnostic tools for two bugs that show up constantly in student life: judging people we don't understand, and telling ourselves that tomorrow will be different. Kabir never wrote about computers, obviously — the "debugging" here is a framing device borrowed from an engineering student's daily vocabulary, not a claim about Kabir himself. What follows is the poetry; the terminal is only the desk it's sitting on.

Who was Kabir?

Kabir was a poet-saint of North India, generally placed in the fifteenth century, associated with the Bhakti (devotional) movement and its Nirguna strand — the belief in a formless God reached through direct inward experience rather than temple ritual, pilgrimage, or scripture. Tradition holds that he lived in Varanasi and worked as a weaver, was likely never formally educated, and composed his verses orally; they were written down and compiled by disciples only after his death. He criticised the ritual excesses of both Hinduism and Islam with equal bluntness, which is part of why his poetry is still sung by Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs alike, and why several of his couplets were later included in the Sikh scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib. His dohas circulate today in large oral and manuscript collections such as the Bijak and the Kabir Granthavali, which is also why exact wording sometimes varies slightly from one printed source to another — a point this project takes seriously rather than glossing over (see the note in Section 4 and the sources list below).

02 — BUG 01 · EXTERNAL BLAME

Judging outward instead of examining inward

Run the scenario below the way you actually would, on an actual day.

📱 timeline · 2 min ago
Someone from your class just posted: "Beyond grateful — starting my internship next month 🎉"

बुरा जो देखन मैं चला, बुरा न मिलिया कोय।
जो दिल खोजा आपना, मुझसे बुरा न कोय॥
Transliteration
Burā jo dekhan maiṅ chalā, burā na miliyā koya;
jo dil khojā āpnā, mujhse burā na koya.
Translation

I set out to find someone bad, and found no one. Then I looked inside my own heart — and found no one worse than myself.

My reading

Kabir isn't performing false modesty here. He's describing an actual experiment: go looking for a villain, and the search itself keeps failing — not because everyone is good, but because "bad" is a much easier word to find in someone else's story than in your own. The couplet doesn't ask you to stop noticing wrong things. It asks you to notice how much faster you find them outward than inward.

On a timeline, this bug is almost invisible because it's disguised as reasonable analysis. "They had connections" sounds like a fact-based explanation, not a judgement — but notice that it's usually offered with no actual information about the person's year, effort, or circumstances. Kabir's doha is uncomfortable precisely because it's aimed at that reflex: the one that explains someone else's win before it explains our own gaps.

03 — INTERSTITIAL

The mirror

no reflection is rendered — there is nothing to see, only somewhere to look

Maybe the hard part of Kabir's advice was never understanding it. It's applying it in the exact moment we're convinced the problem is someone else.

That's what the first doha is actually built on: not an insight about the world, but a redirected gaze. Kabir went looking outward and, finding nothing, was forced to turn the search back on himself. The couplet works the same way on a reader — it opens as an observation about "bad people" and closes as an instruction about you.

04 — BUG 02 · INFINITE POSTPONE

Waiting for a tomorrow that isn't guaranteed

A familiar object appears on screen.

ASSIGNMENTDIGITAL SYSTEMS REPORT
DEADLINETomorrow, 11:59 PM
Status: Not started
ON TRACK

"I'll do it tonight."

काल करे सो आज कर, आज करे सो अब।
पल में प्रलय होएगी, बहुरि करेगा कब॥
Transliteration
Kāl kare so āj kar, āj kare so ab;
pal meṅ pralaya hoegī, bahuri karegā kab.
Translation

Do tomorrow's work today; do today's work now. If the world can end in an instant — when exactly do you plan to do it?

My reading

"Pralaya" literally means cosmic dissolution — the end of the world — which sounds dramatic for a couplet about chores. But that's the point: Kabir isn't exaggerating the stakes of the report, he's pointing out that we quietly assume we have unlimited "laters" to spend, and we don't. The urgency in the doha isn't about productivity. It's about the fact that time is the one resource you can't confirm you'll have more of.

A note on the text

Popular Hindi anthologies print this doha with small spelling variations — प्रलय / परलय for "pralaya," and अब / अब्ब, कब / कब्ब as metrical elongations of "ab" and "kab." The meaning and structure are identical across every version checked for this project; the differences are transmission artifacts typical of a poet whose verses were composed and passed on orally for generations before being fixed in print, not evidence of a different doha. This project uses the more standard modern-Hindi spelling (प्रलय, अब, कब).

Notice that "postpone" never actually removes the task — it just relabels the countdown. That's the small, slightly absurd honesty this bug exposes: the work was never contingent on having more time later, it was contingent on doing it. Kabir's line collapses "tomorrow," "today," and "now" into the same sentence on purpose, because in his logic they were never really three different times to choose between.

06 — A NECESSARY DIGRESSION

But can AI actually understand Kabir?

Since this project uses AI-assisted tools to help build it, it seems dishonest to skip this question.

An AI system — including the one that helped draft parts of this site — can do a specific, useful set of things with a text like a Kabir doha: it can hold the transliteration next to the Devanagari, compare how different translators have rendered "pralaya," notice recurring imagery across dozens of dohas, and generate an explanation in plain English on demand. That's real and it's not nothing.

But understanding a doha like "jo dil khoja aapna" — "when I searched my own heart" — seems to require something an AI doesn't have: a heart to search, a memory of the specific time you assumed the worst about a classmate, a felt discomfort at recognising yourself in the line. Kabir's poems are built to be acted on, not just parsed. A model can tell you the doha is about self-examination. It can't self-examine. That gap is exactly why this project ends with a system that diagnoses but explicitly refuses to fix anything — see the final report below.

09 — AI & CREATIVE TOOLS

How this was made

AI assistance was used for brainstorming the "debugging" framing device, drafting portions of the interpretive text, and generating the HTML/CSS/JavaScript for this page's layout and interactions.

Human-directed / student-decided: the choice of Kabir and these two specific dohas, the overall concept and section structure, the design direction (colour, typography, motion), the interactive flow of each bug, the final wording and edits of every interpretive claim, and the verification of the dohas' text against multiple sources.

AI-assisted: initial code scaffolding for the animations and interactions, and first-draft phrasing for some explanatory paragraphs, which was then reviewed and edited by the student.

No part of Kabir's poetry was AI-generated — the dohas are five-hundred-year-old public-domain text, verified against the sources listed above.
